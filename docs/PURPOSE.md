# What Meno is for

Meno exists to give chemists a research environment in which drawing a
molecule, understanding its three-dimensional structure, testing a hypothesis
by calculation, and taking the next idea from the result are one continuous
line of thought and action rather than separate tasks in separate tools.

At the centre is the molecule, and what the researcher wants to know about it.
A structural formula, a three-dimensional structure, a set of conformers, the
steps of a calculation and the results it produced all belong to the same
subject, and Meno relates them so that it can be taken up from any of those
views without changing tools or losing the thread. The range of conformations
a molecule can adopt matters particularly: being able to work with that range,
and to think about what it means for structure and reactivity, is something
Meno sets out to support rather than to abstract away.

The interface is central to this, not a layer laid over it. Drawing and
handling that match a chemist's intuition; a display that makes state and
change easy to read; a response quick enough that it never interrupts a train
of thought. How it feels to draw a structure, to move it and to compare it is
part of the quality of the instrument, not polish applied to it afterwards.

A procedure that combines several computational methods and outside tools is
expressed in a form the researcher can read, rearrange and run again. Saving
and sharing those procedures along with their results turns a way of working
that depended on one person's experience and handwork into something
reproducible, and something others can build on.

What this is all for is to bring computational chemistry into the everyday
work of understanding molecules and designing syntheses, so that a researcher
can spend their attention on the scientific question, on the interpretation,
and on the next thing worth trying.

**Judge any feature, and any way of implementing one, by how much it serves
that.**
